# Between Inheritance and Arrival

My great-grandfather fought for France in the trenches of the First World War. He survived the gas and returned from the front, but carried the war in his lungs for decades.

The France he defended was not an abstraction. It was made of towns and families, of language passed from parent to child, of inherited ways of life that seemed as permanent as stone.

The France into which I was later born had already been built from different materials.

On my father’s side, Franco-Italian roots had grown deep in French soil, among the historic stone houses of La Rochelle and the green hills of Montmorency. On my mother’s side, a Chinese immigrant family made a life among the concrete towers of the banlieues, alongside other first-generation families.

Stone houses and concrete towers. One side inherited its belonging; the other was building it.

Both were France.

I came from where they met.

Now my grandparents’ generation is gone. Ours has become the living bridge between the worlds they left behind. I too crossed the Atlantic and eventually built a quieter life in Canada.

The movement continued.

So did the inheritance.

Perhaps that is why continuity matters so much to me. My family cannot be divided neatly into the rooted and the immigrant.

Its roots moved.

Arrivals became inheritance, and inheritance crossed borders again.

A country does not endure by standing still. It endures when something essential survives movement: its language, its memory, its obligations, and its ways of recognizing one another.

Borders give a country its form.

Continuity carries its meaning.
