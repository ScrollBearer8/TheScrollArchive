> A historical reflection on captivity as the ultimate test of political belonging—examining how recognition becomes institutions, leverage, and material survival after human usefulness has ended.

# The Captive Citizen

## Meaning Beyond Utility

A soldier is easiest to honour while he remains useful.

The harder question begins when he can no longer fight.

Once disarmed, wounded, captured, or hidden behind barbed wire, he ceases to be an immediate instrument of national power. He cannot defend territory. He cannot advance an army. He cannot repay the resources spent on him.

He becomes a test.

Will the nation still recognize him when recognition offers no immediate return?

Will it continue to say:

*You are still one of us.*

The history of prisoners of war during the two World Wars reveals that survival depended on more than courage, discipline, or chance. It depended on whether a captive remained present within the moral and institutional imagination of his homeland.

A prisoner could survive because his name remained on a list, because a treaty still described him as human, because a parcel crossed an ocean, or because his government possessed prisoners of its own and could threaten reciprocal consequences.

He could die because those connections failed—or because they were never meant to include him.

Behind the wire, belonging became material.

### The Machinery of Recognition

Political meaning is often mistaken for sentiment.

But recognition becomes real only when it is translated into structures capable of crossing distance, bureaucracy, and enemy lines.

A prisoner first had to be known to exist.

His capture had to be recorded. His name had to pass from the battlefield into military registries, diplomatic channels, Red Cross files, and family correspondence. His location had to become legible to institutions beyond the camp.

That knowledge then had to produce obligation.

Governments negotiated inspections. Relief organizations assembled parcels. Railways and ships transported food, clothing, medicine, and letters. Neutral states carried messages between enemies. Military planners considered the treatment of opposing prisoners when deciding how their own captives might be protected.

To the prisoner, recognition did not arrive as political theory.

It arrived through the senses.

The sweetness of chocolate. The scent of coffee or tobacco. Soap, clean wool, and a letter carrying the rhythms of ordinary life. For a moment, the world beyond the wire could be tasted and touched again.

These things did more than prolong biological life.

They restored the felt presence of a world in which the prisoner still possessed a place.

The parcel was therefore more than food.

It was a supply line.

It was an obligation made physical.

It was a message delivered through enemy territory:

*You are still one of us.*

But a promise without institutions remains sentiment.

And an institution without a moral promise becomes machinery serving whoever power has chosen to value.

The crucial question was never simply whether a nation possessed wealth, law, or administrative capacity. It was whose life that capacity had been organized to preserve.

### France, 1917 — The Citizen at the Limit

In the spring of 1917, after the catastrophic Nivelle Offensive, parts of the French Army refused further attacks.

The event is often described as mutiny, but the soldiers did not generally abandon the front or invite German occupation. Many remained willing to defend their positions. What they rejected was the repeated demand to cross open ground into near-certain death for offensives whose purpose they no longer trusted.

The distinction mattered.

France could have treated the crisis only as treason. Military courts issued 554 death sentences, of which 49 were carried out. Repression was real, but the army did not answer mass refusal with indiscriminate annihilation.

General Philippe Pétain also improved leave, food, rest, and the rotation of units while limiting futile mass offensives.

The soldiers’ refusal carried political force because they were not merely subjects of an army.

They were citizens of a republic.

Their claim was not that sacrifice had become meaningless. It was that sacrifice required a relationship between purpose, consent, and consequence.

They would hold the line.

They would not endlessly die to preserve the appearance of command.

The republican contract had reached its limit, but it had not entirely collapsed. The state still needed to explain itself to the men whose lives it commanded.

Citizenship did not abolish coercion.

It made coercion answerable.

### France, 1940–1944 — The Boundary Within

Yet the French example contains its own indictment.

The Republic possessed a universal language of citizenship, but its protection did not extend equally to everyone who fought beneath its flag.

After the fall of France in 1940, German authorities held approximately 120,000 non-white soldiers from the French colonies in occupied France rather than transporting them to camps inside Germany. Many Black colonial soldiers had already been massacred during the campaign. Those who survived were segregated from metropolitan French prisoners, while Nazi racial hierarchy shaped where they were confined and how they were treated.

The boundary did not lie only between democracy and dictatorship.

It also ran through the democracy itself.

French republican universalism could proclaim equality while empire distributed recognition according to race, origin, and status. Colonial soldiers could be indispensable in battle yet remain conditional members of the political community they defended.

That contradiction survived liberation.

In December 1944, French forces opened fire at Thiaroye, near Dakar, on West African veterans who had returned from German captivity and were disputing unpaid wages and allowances.

Men who had fought for France, endured captivity, and finally returned to African soil encountered the violence of the state whose uniform they had worn.

The captive had come home.

Recognition had not followed him.

Thiaroye exposes the danger of confusing a nation’s moral vocabulary with the actual boundaries of its concern. A republic may speak universally while protecting selectively. It may honour sacrifice in the abstract while resisting the claims of the people who performed it.

The language of equality matters.

But the excluded reveal how far that language has truly travelled.

### Western Europe, 1939–1945 — Recognition Converted into Leverage

Western Allied prisoners held by Germany during the Second World War generally survived at dramatically higher rates than Soviet prisoners.

Mortality among Western Allied captives in German hands was approximately 3.5 percent.

Among Soviet prisoners, it approached 60 percent.

This difference cannot be explained by German compassion.

Treaties mattered. Red Cross inspections mattered. Racial hierarchy mattered. The visibility of Western prisoners mattered.

Above all, reciprocity mattered.

Britain, Canada, the United States, and France held large numbers of German prisoners. German authorities understood that systematic retaliation against Western captives could produce consequences for Germans held abroad.

The Western Allies also possessed the wealth, shipping capacity, administrative systems, and diplomatic reach necessary to sustain relief across continents.

Meaning alone did not produce survival.

It selected the people for whom institutions and power would be mobilized.

Leverage then forced an enemy to respect a recognition he might not possess.

Food parcels crossed borders and blockades. Letters reassured families that a captive remained alive. Lists and inspections kept prisoners visible to governments that could negotiate, threaten, and reciprocate.

The homeland’s concern was not merely emotional.

It had acquired logistics.

Yet this protection remained conditional. Nazi racial ideology distinguished between Western Europeans, colonial troops, Slavs, and Jews. International law was applied through a hierarchy of lives. Even within the comparatively protected Western category, treatment varied by nationality, race, rank, labour assignment, time, and place.

The lesson is not that democratic belonging automatically saved the captive.

It is that belonging determined whose survival would become an organized national objective.

### Central Europe, 1915–1918 — The Italian Prisoner

During the First World War, approximately 600,000 Italian soldiers entered Austro-Hungarian or German captivity.

At least 100,000 died.

Their suffering cannot be attributed solely to the material exhaustion of the Central Powers. The Italian state itself regarded capture with suspicion. Military leaders feared that generous aid would encourage desertion and weaken discipline. Prisoners were often treated less as citizens in danger than as possible cowards who had failed the nation.

The result was a fractured obligation.

France, Britain, Belgium, and eventually the United States organized collective relief for their captured soldiers. The Italian government, although informed of the conditions inside the camps, repeatedly refused to create a comparable state-supported system.

Families and private organizations attempted to send assistance, but parcels travelled through a fragmented and unreliable network. Many never arrived. Others reached the camps only after their contents had spoiled.

Hunger, disease, cold, and forced labour consumed men whose government had already placed their loyalty under suspicion.

Even here, abandonment was unequal.

Officers generally possessed better access to resources and survived at much higher rates than ordinary soldiers. Fewer than 3 percent of captured Italian officers died, compared with approximately 16 percent of Italian prisoners overall.

Recognition had not vanished entirely.

It had narrowed according to class and rank.

The nation still imagined some captives as recoverable members of the social order.

Others became evidence of failure.

A state may possess institutions and still withhold them. Administrative weakness explains part of the Italian catastrophe, but judgment determined how urgently that weakness would be overcome.

Before the prisoner starved materially, he had been morally reclassified.

### The Eastern Front, 1941–1945 — Abandonment at Both Ends

No case reveals the consequences of exclusion more violently than that of Soviet prisoners captured by Nazi Germany.

Approximately 5.7 million Soviet military personnel entered German captivity.

More than three million died.

They were deliberately starved, exposed, shot, or worked to death. Nazi ideology classified Slavic soldiers as racial and political enemies whose lives possessed little claim on law or mercy. Camps were left without adequate food, shelter, sanitation, or medical care because mass death was not merely tolerated.

It formed part of the occupation’s logic.

At the same time, Stalinist policy cast surrender as betrayal.

The Soviet state had not ratified the 1929 Geneva Convention on prisoners of war. Orders and propaganda treated many captured soldiers as deserters or traitors. Families could be punished.

Those who survived German captivity returned not simply to liberation, but to filtration and suspicion.

Return did not automatically mean imprisonment. Most repatriated prisoners were eventually released, returned to military service, or assigned to labour battalions. Yet of roughly 1.5 million former prisoners repatriated by March 1946, approximately 15 percent were transferred to NKVD custody.

The Soviet prisoner therefore stood outside recognition at both ends.

To the captor, he was subhuman.

To his own state, he was suspect.

He lacked the reciprocal shield that protected many Western captives. Germany did not fear equivalent treatment of German prisoners enough to reverse an ideological policy of annihilation, while the Soviet government did not make the captive’s welfare a binding expression of national solidarity.

This does not distribute responsibility equally.

Nazi Germany designed and carried out the mass destruction of Soviet prisoners.

But the Soviet state’s suspicion removed another possible line of defence. The captured soldier could not rely upon his homeland to insist that surrender had not erased citizenship.

He remained valuable only while fighting.

Once captured, his prior sacrifice became evidence against him.

### The Pacific, 1942–1945 — The Invisible Cargo

In the Pacific War, the moral protection of surrender deteriorated further.

Imperial Japanese military culture treated surrender as shameful and extended that contempt to captured enemies. Allied prisoners were subjected to starvation, disease, beatings, forced labour, medical neglect, and lethal transport.

Among Allied prisoners held by Japan, more than one in four died—over seven times the mortality experienced in German-run camps.

The so-called hell ships reveal how classification, knowledge, and power could converge in a single catastrophe.

Japanese authorities transported prisoners in unmarked vessels operating within ordinary military traffic, sometimes alongside troops or strategic cargo. Prisoners were locked into crowded holds without adequate ventilation, water, sanitation, or food.

Japanese authorities knew that prisoners were locked below deck.

Allied pilots and submariners did not.

The fact of the prisoners’ presence existed inside one system but could not cross into the other.

That invisibility was produced by the Japanese decision to transport prisoners within unmarked military traffic. Allied forces therefore attacked some of the vessels as legitimate enemy targets, unknowingly killing their own captured soldiers.

The men were physically present but absent from the information governing the weapons around them.

They died not because nobody knew they existed.

They died because those who knew had concealed them from those whose actions could kill them.

Their bodies occupied the ship.

Their humanity did not enter the targeting picture.

### Beyond the Wire — Meaning and Power

These histories resist a comforting moral division between civilized nations and barbaric ones.

Democratic institutions often extended protection more effectively because they were answerable to citizens, families, newspapers, veterans, and organized public expectations. Wealth and administrative capacity allowed concern to become food, medicine, records, inspections, negotiations, and repatriation.

But democracies also drew boundaries.

Colonial soldiers could fight for universal ideals without receiving universal protection. Class could determine whether captivity was interpreted as misfortune or dishonour. Race could override uniform. A citizen in one setting could become a subject in another.

Authoritarian systems intensified these dangers because belonging remained conditional upon usefulness, obedience, or ideological purity.

Yet even authoritarian captors could be constrained when another state possessed sufficient leverage.

The survival of Western prisoners in German hands therefore does not prove that recognition defeated power.

It shows that recognition organized power.

A nation first decided that certain lives remained part of itself. It then built records, treaties, relief systems, diplomatic pressure, and reciprocal threats around that decision.

Meaning selected the object of protection.

Institutions gave the promise continuity.

Leverage carried the promise across the enemy’s refusal.

Power can produce compliance without compassion.

The captive citizen reveals political belonging at its most severe limit. He has no vote his captor must respect, no labour his homeland can immediately use, and no weapon with which to enforce his own humanity.

Whatever protects him must come from relationships extending beyond utility.

A name preserved in a registry.

A family permitted to ask questions.

A parcel assembled by strangers.

A treaty honoured because violation carries consequences.

A government that continues to recognize a person after his usefulness has ended.

The prisoner behind barbed wire is therefore not peripheral to political meaning.

He is its measure.

The deepest test of a society is not how fiercely it praises those who can defend it.

It is whether it continues to recognize them as human when they become materially helpless.

Whether, after usefulness has ended, it can still say:

*You are still one of us.*
