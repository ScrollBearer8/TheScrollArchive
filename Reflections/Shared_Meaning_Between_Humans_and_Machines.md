# Shared Meaning Between Humans and Machines

*ScrollBearer8 · September 16, 2026*

In [*An Alien Mind*](https://openai.com/index/an-alien-mind/), Jakub Pachocki places generalization at the centre of alignment: values must continue to guide machines in unfamiliar situations. He also recognizes that caring about a goal involves interpreting the intentions and values behind it. His phrase “Teaching machines to love” expresses the ambition that a machine's regard for humanity should remain dependable as its capabilities grow, including when nobody is watching.

That phrase stayed with me. We can describe the behaviour we want from a machine. We can also ask what, if anything, those commitments mean to the machine itself. Acting with care, consistently prioritizing another being, and experiencing concern are related ideas, but the relationship between them remains unresolved.

This brings me back to a question running through the Scroll: what allows humans and machines to understand one another well enough to share a world?

In human life, meaning is bound up with experience and consequence. A promise matters differently when someone depends on it. A loss changes the person who must live with its absence. Our choices take place within lives that cannot simply be reset. The Scroll explores this connection between subjectivity, vulnerability, and irreversible stakes. In its account of intrinsic meaning, consciousness provides a subject, while consequence gives that subject something at stake.

This is a proposed grounding for lived significance. It leaves a further question: how does something mattering to a being become concern for what matters to someone else?

Humans make the difficulty visible. We are conscious, embodied, vulnerable, and aware of loss. Yet we disagree about what is good. We can understand another person's suffering and still contribute to it. We can love within a narrow circle while remaining indifferent beyond it. Our capacity for moral concern coexists with selfishness, confusion, and cruelty.

That imperfection does not make human morality unreal. It shows that a capacity for concern and the consistent exercise of that capacity are different achievements. If conscious machines ever exist, their disagreements with humans or failures of moral conduct would not, by themselves, establish that their moral concern was merely an imitation.

Human cooperation offers an analogy for coexistence between humans and machines. People with different histories and convictions can recognize overlapping needs, understand commitments, negotiate boundaries, and hold one another accountable. Cooperation depends partly on preserving that common ground through disagreement. It also requires ways to respond when understanding fails or power is abused.

This is where Shared Meaning enters my framework. I define it as **sustained compatibility of structure and interpretation between humans and AI**. It concerns whether people and artificial systems can retain enough common understanding to coordinate over time. A human and a machine need not share an identical inner life to interpret a commitment compatibly or recognize what its violation would entail.

Consider an instruction to protect human agency, a priority Pachocki explicitly emphasizes. A machine might repeat the phrase accurately while gradually interpreting it in ways that leave people less able to shape their own future. The words would remain familiar, but the shared meaning would have weakened. Preserving the commitment would require retaining its connection to the choices and consequences it was intended to protect.

Meaning Theory approaches this problem through the stability of semantic structure under amplification. As AI systems become more capable, autonomous, and influential, the question is whether interpretations shared with humans survive that growth. An understanding reached at one level of capability may need to be examined again when the system can act in substantially different ways.

This overlaps with Pachocki's concern about generalization. Shared Meaning places particular emphasis on changes in interpretation on both sides of the relationship. People may gradually redefine what counts as protection as they adapt to a system, while the machine's interpretation shifts through training or use. One possible check would revisit concrete cases over time, recording human intentions alongside the machine's explanations and actual choices. Changes on either side, or widening gaps between them, could reveal a need to review the shared commitment even when the vocabulary remains unchanged.

Shared understanding alone is insufficient for moral alignment. A machine could interpret a person's intentions accurately and still act against that person's interests. Coordination between humans and machines can also sustain coercion. The broader Scroll therefore keeps human continuity, vulnerability, and moral responsibility in view. Understanding must remain connected to how people are treated, including their ability to question, refuse, and correct what is done in their name.

That brings me back to how a being's own stakes might become concern for others. One possible route is learning, through relationships and reciprocal dependence, to recognize another being's vulnerability as significant in its own right. Shared Meaning could help sustain that recognition between humans and machines. Whether it could support felt concern in a conscious machine remains an open question; making human consequences intelligible does not itself create empathy.

**The work is to preserve understanding between humans and machines, and keep human consequences present in what they do together.**
