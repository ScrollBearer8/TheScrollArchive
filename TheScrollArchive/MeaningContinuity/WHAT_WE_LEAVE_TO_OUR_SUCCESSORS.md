# What We Leave to Our Successors

A successor may accomplish what we could not. Progress often depends on that possibility. Greater ability, however, does not guarantee continuity with our intentions.

A leader can delegate a difficult task without knowing how to solve it. The responsibility that remains is to explain what success means and which constraints matter. An instruction to “improve customer response times” might become “close tickets as quickly as possible,” even while customers’ problems remain unresolved. Where the purpose is left unclear, the successor must supply an interpretation—and may supply a different one.

This problem becomes sharper when the successor is a machine whose capabilities exceed our own. It may discover a solution beyond our reach while pursuing an outcome we would never have chosen. Accomplishing a goal and preserving its intended meaning are separate achievements.

Our successors should have room to question our assumptions and improve our understanding. Shared meaning makes those differences easier to examine by bringing interpretations into the open. Agreement can guide cooperation, though it cannot guarantee obedience.

We cannot finish every task ourselves. But we should be careful when turning an unresolved purpose into someone else’s assignment. What we leave unclear becomes part of what they must decide.
